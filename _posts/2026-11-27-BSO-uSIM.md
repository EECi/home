  <div class="textBlock">
    This week, Josh, Nmeso and I swapped the flat fens for Glasgow and <a href="https://www.bso-usim2026.org/">BSO-uSIM 2026</a>, the first joint conference of IBPSA-England and IBPSA-Scotland, hosted by the University of Strathclyde at its Technology and Innovation Centre. The theme was <em>Simulating retrofit at scale: supporting net zero upgrading of communities and urban areas</em>. Over two days, around fifty papers covered stock modelling, calibration and uncertainty, heat networks, retrofit policy and the people who live and work in the buildings being upgraded. EECi presented three of them, each looking at a different scale: a hospital campus, the housing stock of a London borough, and a university estate.  </div>

    <h3>Screening a partially metered estate</h3>
    <!-- TODO: replace # with the link to Paper 105 -->
    <a class="paperLink" href="#">Read the paper →</a>
    My paper started from a practical question that estate teams face all the time: with far more buildings than you can examine, where should the limited audit time, controls review or simulation effort go? On an acute hospital campus there are usually two kinds of evidence. Annual accounts cover everything but say nothing about <em>when</em> energy is used. Half-hourly sub-meters show that behaviour, but they only cover part of the estate and don't always map cleanly onto buildings. In one example from the talk, metered electricity and the certificates for 18 units agreed to within 6% in total, yet only 8 of the 18 agreed individually to within ±30%.    <br><br>
    Working with 19 heating and 44 electricity metered units over 2021 to 2026, the workflow combines both sources. Demand is concentrated in a few units: seven heating and eleven electricity units account for 80% of each. Electricity units fall into base-load-dominated and daytime-scheduled groups, while heating looks more like a continuous base-load gradient than separate types. The output is a shortlist that names which units need an audit, a check of the metering, or a detailed model, and gives the next step for each. Or, as my closing slide put it: check the meter before you calibrate the building.  </div>
</div>

    <h3>Who gets left behind?</h3>
    <!-- TODO: replace # with the link to Paper 134 -->
    <a class="paperLink" href="#">Read the paper →</a>
    Nmeso's paper moved from estates to homes, and from my question (where did all the electricity go?) to a more important one (who gets help paying for it?). The Warm Homes Plan rightly prioritises fuel-poor households, but it may miss a large group: households above the low-income thresholds that still can't afford to pay for retrofit themselves and, as renters, can't use owner-occupier grants. Applying EnergyFlex, a household-level urban building energy model, to the London Borough of Haringey, Nmeso estimated each household's income, applied the main Warm Homes Plan eligibility routes to the synthetic population, and calculated net retrofit costs and post-retrofit bills for each household.
    <br><br>
    The results are stark. Fewer than a third of in-scope households qualify for support, and around 37% of the borough would have to self-fund. The excluded group is concentrated among private renters in mid-efficiency homes. As one of Nmeso's slides put it, the disparities are a design choice: where the thresholds sit and how income is defined decide who is left out. This connects directly to Nmeso's <a href="{{ '/CSaP-PIF/' | relative_url }}">CSaP-funded interview project</a> with households in this missing middle.

  <div class="textBlock">
    <h3>Estate-scale energy diagnostics</h3>
    <!-- TODO: replace # with the link to Paper 157 -->
    <a class="paperLink" href="#">Read the paper →</a>
    Josh's paper brought us back to estates, tackling a similar screening problem at university scale, using hourly electricity data from 121 buildings on the University of Cambridge estate. After cleaning the data and normalising it by floor area, Josh calculated operational metrics for each building (energy use intensity, baseload intensity, peak load intensity and load factor) and grouped buildings with a two-stage clustering method. The first stage finds operational archetypes; the second groups them into cohorts across the estate for interventions that depend on location, such as solar deployment and electrification.
    <br><br>
    One finding stood out: a building's declared function is a poor predictor of how it actually uses energy, which argues for intervention strategies built on behaviour rather than use class. Baseload intensity also tracked overall energy intensity closely. The paper also identified gaps in ISO standards for research glasshouses, plant growth chambers and cold storage, where better benchmarks could speed up efficiency improvements.
  </div>
</div>

<div class="textRow">
  <div class="textBlock">
    All three papers share a thread that ran through much of the conference: before you simulate in detail, work out where simulation is worth doing and what decision it will inform. Thank you to Prof Nick Kelly, Prof Neveen Hamza and the team at Strathclyde for organising such a well-run first joint conference, to IES for sponsoring it, and to everyone who came to our respective sessions with good questions. The papers will be published in the conference proceedings on the IBPSA website. All three of us stuck (more or less) to our twelve-minute slots, and we finished the trip with a well-earned dinner in Glasgow, where the only thing we metered was the pints.

document.querySelectorAll('.carousel').forEach(function (carousel) {
  var track = carousel.querySelector('.carouselTrack');
  var slides = track.querySelectorAll('.carouselSlide');
  if (slides.length < 2) return;

  function makeBtn(cls, label, text) {
    var b = document.createElement('button');
    b.type = 'button';
    b.className = 'carouselBtn ' + cls;
    b.setAttribute('aria-label', label);
    b.textContent = text;
    carousel.appendChild(b);
    return b;
  }

  var prev = makeBtn('carouselPrev', 'Previous photo', '‹');
  var next = makeBtn('carouselNext', 'Next photo', '›');

  var dotsWrap = document.createElement('div');
  dotsWrap.className = 'carouselDots';
  var dots = Array.prototype.map.call(slides, function (_, i) {
    var d = document.createElement('button');
    d.type = 'button';
    d.className = 'carouselDot';
    d.setAttribute('aria-label', 'Show photo ' + (i + 1) + ' of ' + slides.length);
    d.addEventListener('click', function () { go(i); });
    dotsWrap.appendChild(d);
    return d;
  });
  carousel.appendChild(dotsWrap);

  function current() {
    return Math.round(track.scrollLeft / track.clientWidth);
  }

  function go(i) {
    i = Math.max(0, Math.min(slides.length - 1, i));
    track.scrollTo({ left: i * track.clientWidth });
  }

  function update() {
    var i = current();
    dots.forEach(function (d, j) { d.setAttribute('aria-current', j === i ? 'true' : 'false'); });
    prev.disabled = i === 0;
    next.disabled = i === slides.length - 1;
  }

  prev.addEventListener('click', function () { go(current() - 1); });
  next.addEventListener('click', function () { go(current() + 1); });
  track.addEventListener('scroll', function () { window.requestAnimationFrame(update); });
  window.addEventListener('resize', update);
  update();
});
</script>
